# Product Discovery

Product discovery is how a team learns what is worth building: who the customers are, what they struggle with, which solutions could help, and whether those solutions will actually work, before committing to build them. Done well it is a small, steady habit rather than a big project. This manual covers the whole cycle and gives concrete steps, questions, rules of thumb and templates.

## How to apply this skill

1. **Find out where the person is.** Typical starting points: no clear outcome yet; no customer contact; interviews but no synthesis; many ideas but no tests; a decision to defend to stakeholders. Start at that step, not at the beginning.
2. **Produce usable artifacts, not only advice.** Interview guides (see the fast path below), interview snapshots, opportunity trees, assumption lists, test plans and stakeholder updates. Templates are in `references/templates.md`.
3. **Ask what you need before producing anything that depends on it**, in a short numbered list with a default for each question, and use what the person has already shared. Never ask for what they already told you.
4. **Correct common mistakes as they appear** (asking customers what to build, leading or hypothetical questions, tests with no success criteria, testing an idea instead of an assumption). Give the reason in a sentence so the person can apply it next time.
5. **Be honest about evidence strength.** Say plainly when a conclusion rests on opinions, a handful of conversations, or a test that couldn't have failed.
6. **Adapt to the context.** Small teams, no customers yet, regulated industries and heavy-process organizations need adjusted versions of these practices. See `references/pitfalls-and-limits.md`.

## Fast path: a request to prepare an interview

When the request is to prepare a conversation with a customer, user or expert (often a single sentence describing the project and who will be interviewed), do not walk through the whole workflow. Read `references/interview-guide-builder.md` and follow its two steps:

1. **Gather context first.** For a new project, reply with a short list of questions (at most five, each with a default) about the essentials the request left out: the project and the decision it feeds, who the participant is, what is already known, what the person most wants to learn, and logistics only if they matter. Do not write the guide yet, and include no draft questions in this reply. Skip this step only if the request already covers the essentials, the project context was given earlier in the conversation, or the person asks for a draft right away.
2. **Write the complete guide.** Once they answer (or say to use defaults), read `references/example-interview-guide.md` once to calibrate depth, then deliver a ready-to-use guide: learning goals, beliefs to challenge, a timed run sheet of story-based prompts with probes, reminders for the interviewer and an after-meeting checklist. Keep every prompt about specific past behavior, keep the idea out of the conversation, list assumptions at the top, and offer to adjust.

Write in the language of the request.

## Six principles

1. **Start with a goal and a question.** Know the outcome you are pursuing and the few things you most need to learn before you research anything. Research without a question produces anecdotes. Do desk research first; never ask people for what you could look up.
2. **Customers don't design solutions.** They know their problems and their lives, not what to build. Never ask what to build, whether they would use something, or what they would pay. Treat feature requests as symptoms of an underlying need.
3. **Learn, don't validate.** Look for evidence that could prove you wrong, ask the uncomfortable question, expect many ideas to fail, and treat bad news as useful: it is the cheapest kind.
4. **Behavior beats opinion.** Trust specific past behavior, observed actions and real commitments over stated intentions. Compliments, hypotheticals and polite agreement cost people nothing, so they carry little information.
5. **Keep it small, cheap, fast and ongoing.** Do the least research that gives the confidence you need. Some beats none. Raise cost and rigor only as risk grows. Discovery is not a phase that ends, and months of conversation with no testing is also a failure.
6. **Involve the whole team and share raw evidence.** The people who build the product, including engineers, should hear customers directly. No single person should filter what customers say.

## The workflow

The steps are cyclical, not linear. Teams move back and forth as they learn.

### 1. Set an outcome and learning goals
- Anchor discovery to one desired outcome: a change in customer behavior that drives business results. Prefer an outcome the team can influence over a lagging business metric.
- Write the top three things you need to learn, and what answer would change a decision.
- Agree in advance how confident you need to be, scaled to the cost of being wrong.
- Details: `references/framing.md`

### 2. Map what you already know
- Each team member sketches how customers currently get to the outcome (an experience map); merge the sketches into one.
- Define a narrow target segment and screen participants for it.
- Details: `references/framing.md`

### 3. Interview customers every week
- At least one interview a week, continuously, with the whole team present.
- Ask for specific recent stories, not opinions or predictions. Don't pitch.
- Write a one-page snapshot after each interview and share it.
- Details: `references/interviewing.md`

### 4. Map and choose opportunities
- Turn what you hear into a tree of customer needs, pains and desires (an opportunity solution tree), worded from the customer's perspective.
- Compare sibling opportunities one level at a time; choose, then go deeper.
- Details: `references/opportunities-and-ideas.md`

### 5. Generate and narrow solutions
- Everyone generates ideas alone first, then share, then narrow to about three candidates.
- Always compare several options.
- Details: `references/opportunities-and-ideas.md`

### 6. Surface assumptions
- Test assumptions, not ideas. Walk through the idea step by step and list what must be true.
- Classify (desirability, usability, feasibility, viability, ethical) and pick the important ones with the least evidence.
- Details: `references/assumptions-and-testing.md`

### 7. Test the riskiest assumptions
- Set success criteria before the test. Simulate the experience so people act instead of speculate. Start with the cheapest test that could prove you wrong.
- Details: `references/assumptions-and-testing.md`

### 8. Decide, share and repeat
- Compare options, decide, and record what you learned. Show stakeholders the whole path, not just the conclusion.
- Details: `references/stakeholders-and-habits.md`

## Quick guide

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| Don't know what to focus on | Define the product outcome; draw experience maps |
| Don't know customers' real problems | Weekly story interviews; write snapshots |
| Customer praises your idea | Thank them; ask how they handle it today |
| Customer says "I would..." | Ask when they last did it and what happened |
| Customer requests a feature | Ask for the last time they needed it; find the underlying need |
| Too many needs or ideas | Build an opportunity solution tree; compare siblings |
| Stuck choosing | Compare on size, market, company and customer; no scoring; remember the choice is reversible |
| Need solutions | Ideate alone, share, dot-vote down to about three |
| Afraid of building the wrong thing | List assumptions; test the riskiest ones first |
| Need to know if interest is real | Test with commitments of time, reputation or money |
| Need early evidence cheaply | Use the lowest-fidelity test on the test ladder |
| Stakeholders push solutions | Show the tree and the evidence; compare options |
| Answers are inconsistent | Narrow the segment |
| Unsure whether you've done enough | Success criteria met, patterns repeating, or the team is comfortable acting |
| No time or support | One interview a week; share what you learn |
