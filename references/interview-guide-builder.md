# Building an interview guide from a short request

Use this when someone asks for an interview guide, script or preparation for a conversation with a customer, user or other person, often in one sentence ("help me prepare a first meeting with a personal trainer who works with older adults"). The goal is a guide the person can print and carry into the meeting, not an essay about interviewing.

## Work from what was said

The person may be interviewing alone. Write "who asks and who takes notes" so it works for one person (suggest recording with permission) and do not assume a team. Mention the person's own profession only if it changes the guide.

### Step 1: gather context before writing (new projects)

The first message about a new project rarely holds enough to write a guide that fits. A generic guide wastes the person's time in front of a real participant, so do not write the guide straight away. Reply first with a short set of questions about whatever essential information is missing. Keep the rest of the guide for the next turn.

Skip this step, and write the guide immediately, only when one of these is true: the request already covers the essentials below; the person has already given context about this project earlier in the conversation; or the person asks to skip questions ("just give me a draft"). In that case state your assumptions at the top of the guide and invite correction at the end.

**Essentials to have before writing.** Ask only about those the request does not already answer:

1. **The project and the decision.** What is being built or explored, for whom, and what decision will these conversations feed? (If there is no idea yet, only a problem area, say so.)
2. **The participant.** Who exactly they are, how they relate to the target customer (end user, buyer, professional who serves the customer, expert), and whether the person already knows them.
3. **What is already known.** Prior interviews or research, current hypotheses, and any existing idea or prototype.
4. **What the person most wants to learn** from this conversation, and anything off limits or sensitive.
5. **Logistics, only if they would change the guide:** length, in person or remote, interviewing alone or with a note-taker, language, recording.

**How to ask.**
- Open with one line that shows what you understood from the request.
- Ask at most five numbered questions, ordered by importance, each short and answerable in a sentence. Leave out any the request already answers.
- Offer a default for each ("if you don't know, I'll assume ..."), so the person can reply "defaults" and move on. State the logistics defaults (45 minutes, video call, interviewing alone, first meeting is exploratory) in one line instead of asking about them.
- Say what you will produce once they answer, in one line.
- Do not include a draft guide, sample questions or advice about interviewing in this reply.
- Use the language of the request.

After the person answers, or says to use defaults, go straight to the guide. Do not ask a second round unless an answer reveals a gap that would change the guide's core. Later requests in the same project (another participant, a follow-up interview) reuse the context already gathered; ask only about what is new.

### Step 2: write the complete guide

Infer anything still missing, list the assumptions in a short line at the top, and invite correction at the end. After delivering, offer at most two or three targeted refinements (for example "Is the person a solo practitioner or part of an organization?").

Write the guide in the language of the request. Use plain, natural wording a participant would use in conversation, and the participant's own vocabulary for their field.

## Read the request

Extract or infer:

| Item | If not stated |
|---|---|
| Project, idea or area being explored | Infer the domain; treat any idea as a belief to test, not something to present |
| Who the participant is | Infer role, and decide whether they are an end user, buyer, influencer, professional serving the target customer, or expert |
| Stage | "First meeting" means exploratory. Otherwise assume problem discovery |
| Length | 45 minutes (60 if two core stories are planned) |
| Format | Video call or in person |
| Language and region | Same as the request |
| Participant's relationship to the person asking | Assume a stranger who is doing a favor |

## Choose the interview type

- **First or exploratory conversation (default).** Learn the participant's world: their work or daily life, the last time specific things happened, how they cope today, what it costs, who decides. Keep any product idea out of the room. If the person has an idea, convert it into beliefs to test indirectly and into things to listen for.
- **Problem follow-up.** Go deep on one need or moment that earlier conversations surfaced. Use the same story format, narrower.
- **Solution or concept test.** Show a prototype or description only after the problem is understood. Ask the participant to do a task or to commit time, introductions or money, rather than rate it. Plan this with `assumptions-and-testing.md`.
- **Expert or stakeholder conversation.** Ask for their experience and the evidence behind it, what they have seen fail, and who else to speak to.

## Adjust for the participant's role

- **End user (including a professional who is the user of the tool being explored, such as a teacher exploring grading tools):** their own recent behavior, tools, workarounds and frustrations. If they also talk about their customers or students, treat that as second-hand.
- **Buyer or budget holder:** how the last purchase was decided, who was involved, alternatives considered, the budget source, what blocked or sped up the decision.
- **Professional who serves the target customers (a trainer, teacher, nurse, adviser):** use two lenses. First, their own work as a user: how they find clients, plan, record, communicate, get paid. Second, specific past clients ("tell me about the last client who stopped"). A second-hand account is less reliable than a first-hand one and should be treated as a lead; plan to talk to the end customers directly too. Keep client details anonymous.
- **Expert:** patterns across many cases, but still ask for concrete examples.

## Structure of the guide

Produce these parts, in this order. Aim for two to three printed pages: keep every part tight, use the table for the run sheet, and cut probes and reminders before cutting learning goals or the run sheet.

1. **Header.** Purpose of the conversation in one sentence, participant profile, duration, format, who asks and who takes notes, and the assumptions made.
2. **Learning goals.** The top three things to learn, each with the decision it would inform.
3. **Beliefs to challenge.** What the team currently assumes, written so it can be wrong, plus what answer would surprise you. These are for the interviewer only.
4. **Before the meeting.** A short checklist: homework done, consent and recording permission, notes template ready, the small ask for the end (introductions, a follow-up conversation).
5. **Run sheet.** A table of blocks with minutes. For each block: its purpose, the main prompt, follow-up probes, and what to listen for. Use this skeleton and rename blocks to fit the domain:
   - Opening (3 to 5 min): thank them, say what you are doing and that you are not selling, ask permission to take notes or record, and say there are no right answers.
   - Context (5 to 8 min): their role and a recent typical week, kept brief and tied to specifics.
   - Core story 1 (12 to 20 min): the last time the key situation happened, told in sequence.
   - Core story 2 (8 to 15 min): a contrasting case (a time it went badly, a customer who left, a task they abandoned).
   - Tools, workarounds and cost (5 to 8 min): what they use or invented, what it costs in time and money, and what they tried and dropped.
   - Money and decisions (3 to 6 min): who pays, who decides, how the last choice was made.
   - Wrap-up (3 to 5 min): "Is there anything I should have asked?", an introduction to someone else, and a small next step.
6. **In-the-moment reminders.** A few lines: stay on one specific event, no pitching, silence is fine, and how to answer the three kinds of bad data (compliment, hypothetical, feature request). See `interviewing.md`.
7. **After the meeting.** Write the snapshot the same day, debrief, mark which learning goals were answered, and decide who to interview next. Point to the snapshot template in `templates.md`.

Add an optional **pocket card** (five or six key prompts on a few lines) when the person will be in person and may not want to carry the whole guide.

## Timing

Block minutes must add up to the stated duration minus three to five minutes of slack. A 45 minute conversation holds roughly two core stories at most. If there are more topics than time, cut topics, not depth. Mark the blocks that can be dropped first.

## Sensitive contexts

- **Health, age, money, family or other personal topics.** Ask about activities, adaptations and decisions, not diagnoses or finances in detail. Let the participant decide how much to share, and say so.
- **Older or less technical participants.** Offer a phone call or an in-person meeting, use plain language, a slower pace and shorter questions, and avoid screen-sharing unless it is needed.
- **Clients or patients described by a professional.** Ask them not to name or identify anyone.
- **Recording.** Ask permission and explain how the notes will be used.
- **Regulated fields.** Keep to behavior and process; avoid asking for confidential information.

## Quality check before delivering

Review the guide against this list and fix anything that fails.

- Every main prompt asks about a specific past event, a fact or a current behavior. No prompt asks for a prediction, an opinion of an idea, or a price.
- No prompt describes or hints at the solution.
- No more than ten main prompts for 45 to 60 minutes (one per block plus the opening and closing); probes are optional.
- Each learning goal is covered by at least one block.
- At least one prompt could show the team's belief is wrong.
- Wording matches the participant's vocabulary and the language of the request.
- The timing adds up.
- The assumptions line and the closing offer to refine are present.
