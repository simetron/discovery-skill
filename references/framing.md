## Choose one outcome

An outcome is a change in customer behavior that drives business results. Output (a feature shipped) is not an outcome; it only matters if it changes behavior.

| Level | What it is | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Business outcome | Lagging result such as revenue or retention. Hard for one team to move directly | Context and sanity check |
| Product outcome | Leading behavior the team can influence, such as the share of new users who complete a key action | The target for discovery |
| Traction metric | Usage of a specific feature | Only when optimizing a known solution or for newer teams. It locks the team into one solution |

- Check the link: why would moving the product outcome move the business outcome? If you cannot say, pick another outcome.
- Keep the same people on the same outcome long enough to learn. Several teams sharing one business outcome invites blame instead of learning.
- If the team is new to this, start with a learning goal (for example, "understand why new users stop after week one") before a numeric target. Specific numeric targets only motivate when the team believes they are reachable.
- A poorly chosen outcome undermines everything downstream, so test it with stakeholders before building on it.

## Write your learning goals

Before every study or conversation:
1. Write your top three unknowns.
2. For each, note what answer would change a decision. If none would, drop it.
3. Do desk research first. Don't ask people for what you can look up.
4. Learn the organization's goals and constraints and how it makes decisions. Talk to stakeholders early; the research may show the original problem was the wrong one.

Optional framing questions for a larger effort: What business objective does this serve? How will we measure success? What customer problem are we solving? Who is the target market?

If you cannot say what you want to learn, postpone the conversation.

## Decide how much is enough

Agree beforehand how confident you need to be, and scale it to the cost of being wrong. Certainty is never available.

- Qualitative work (interviews, observation): you have enough when the same patterns keep recurring.
- Quantitative work (surveys, experiments): you need an adequate sample and statistical significance.
- The bigger and less reversible the investment, the more evidence to ask for.

## Map the customer experience

An experience map is a picture of how customers currently go about reaching the outcome: the moments, actions and events, connected in order.

1. Agree on the scope around your outcome.
2. Each team member draws their own map, alone.
3. Study each other's maps.
4. Merge into one: include all steps, collapse similar ones, map beyond the happy path, and add context.

Use the map to decide what to ask about in interviews, and refine it after each round of learning.

## Define who you are learning from

- Segment customers by shared problems and goals. If interview answers are all over the place, the segment is too broad: slice it into smaller groups and compare.
- Prefer customers who are reachable, worth serving and pleasant to work with.
- For marketplaces, treat each side as its own segment.
- Use screening questions so participants match the people you are building for. Avoid convenience samples.
- Base your picture of customers on observed behavior, not invented personas.
