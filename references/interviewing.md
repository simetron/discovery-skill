# Interviewing customers

Weekly interviews are the habit that powers the rest of discovery. Their purpose is to find needs, pains and desires, never to ask people what to build.

## Cadence and people

- Hold at least one interview a week, continuously, including after you have chosen an opportunity to work on. Regular contact keeps assumptions fresh; occasional research projects don't.
- The whole product team attends (for example product manager, designer and engineer) so everyone hears the customer directly. One person asks; the others listen and take notes. A session of about an hour is a good default.
- A solo person can still do this. Share raw notes with someone who will challenge your conclusions.

## Recruiting

- Automate it so it does not depend on one person's effort: in-product prompts, invitations triggered by events (a cancellation, a support request, a customization request), customer-facing colleagues, an advisory group.
- Warm introductions beat cold outreach. Go where customers gather (communities, meetups, conferences), or give them a reason to come to you (events, useful content, a landing page with a sign-up).
- Screen participants so they match your target segment. Don't talk to whoever happens to be available.
- Be honest about why you are asking. Do not invent cover stories.
- Keep going until you stop learning new things about a question, and keep interviewing weekly anyway.

## Prepare

- Write your top three unknowns (see `framing.md`).
- Look up what you can. Learn about the person and their business.
- Draft story prompts around the moments on your experience map.
- If you do not know what you want to learn, postpone the conversation.

## Ask for stories

Memory and self-description are unreliable, and people describe the person they wish they were. Specific recent events are far more accurate than generalities.

- Ask: "Tell me about the last time you [did the thing]."
- Walk through it in order: what happened first, what happened next, what did you do, what did you use, how did it end.
- Don't let them generalize ("I usually...", "I always..."). Pull them back to the specific time.
- Prefer story prompts to abstract "how do you usually" or "why do you" questions. Use "why" sparingly, as a short follow-up inside a specific story.

## Question bank

| Purpose | Prompts |
|---|---|
| Open | "Tell me about the last time you [did X]." "What was going on when you decided to do that?" |
| Sequence | "What happened right before that?" "What did you do next?" "Then what?" |
| Detail | "Who else was involved?" "What tools did you use?" "Show me how you did it." |
| Alternatives | "What else did you try?" "What did you use before?" "How did you find it?" |
| Cost and money | "How long did that take?" "What did it cost you?" "Where does the budget for this come from?" |
| Priorities | "What else was competing for your attention that week?" "What are your top priorities right now?" |
| Close | "Who else should I talk to?" "Is there anything I should have asked?" |

Questions that mislead (avoid): "Is my idea good?", "Would you use or buy this?", "How much would you pay?", "Do you ever...?", anything that describes your solution and asks for an opinion.

## During the conversation

- Talk less and leave silences. The customer owns the problem; you are there to learn it.
- Don't pitch. If you catch yourself explaining your idea, stop and return to their life.
- Confirm the problem matters to them before digging into detail. If they never do the activity, they may invent reasons rather than admit indifference. A lukewarm reaction is data: ask what is behind it.
- Look for workarounds. A homemade solution is strong evidence of a real pain.
- In business settings, ask where the money for this comes from.
- Ask at least one question that could show your idea is wrong.
- Keep it casual and, if possible, in person. Tell them you are taking notes and keep eye contact.

## Handling bad data

| Bad data | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Compliment | "That sounds great" | Thank them, then ask how they handle it today |
| Fluff or hypothetical | "I would...", "I always...", "I might..." | Ask for the last real time it happened, step by step |
| Idea or feature request | "You should add X" | Ask for the last time they needed it and what they did without it. Find the underlying need. A series of requests is often one need in disguise |

Negative feedback is valuable: it stops you from over-investing.

## Capture and share

- Within the day, write a one-page interview snapshot (template in `templates.md`): customer facts and segment, opportunities (needs, pains, desires) in the customer's own words, key insights, memorable exact quotes, and a mini experience map.
- Record exact quotes, emotions and life context, not only conclusions.
- Debrief with the team right after the session.
- Share snapshots and raw quotes with the whole team, not just a summary, so no one person becomes the filter for what customers said.

## Framing a request for a conversation

When you are asking someone for their time as a favor:
1. State the problem you are working on (not your idea).
2. Say where you are in the process.
3. Admit what you lack.
4. Explain why this person can help (do your homework first).
5. Make a specific, small ask.

## Other ways to see customers' reality

- Observe or shadow customers at work, or do the task by hand yourself, to learn the problem first-hand.
- Watch for off-label uses of your product; they reveal unmet needs.
- If you are building for businesses, a small group of real, willing customers who agree to review prototypes can speed learning (roughly five to eight for business products, more for consumer products). If you cannot recruit them, the problem may not be strong enough.
