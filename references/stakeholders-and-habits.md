# Deciding, sharing and building the habit

## Contents
- Decide and record
- Share the journey with stakeholders
- Build the weekly habit
- Start small

## Decide and record

- Compare options and decide; most product decisions are reversible, so don't wait for perfect data.
- When a test fails, write down what you learned. A failed test that stops you from building the wrong thing is a success.
- Decide in advance what each test outcome means (persevere, adjust or stop) so results lead to action.
- Measure only what you need to evaluate your tests and track your outcome. Improve instrumentation gradually.

## Share the journey with stakeholders

Stakeholders are people who can block or support the work. Treat them as partners.

- Understand their constraints and goals, and look for solutions that work for them as well as customers.
- Show the whole path, not only the conclusion: the outcome, the opportunities you found, the options you considered, and the evidence for each. The tree is a good visual for this.
- Don't lead with a solution, and don't overwhelm people with detail.
- Debate evidence, not opinions. Where you disagree, ask what evidence would change each person's mind.
- Meet people one-on-one during discovery. Large group meetings turn into design by committee.
- Share learnings regularly and openly, so nothing at the end is a surprise.
- Don't expect to win the argument in a single conversation.

## Build the weekly habit

A simple rhythm that keeps discovery alive:
1. **Every week:** at least one customer interview with the whole team present. Snapshot the same day.
2. **Every week:** a short team review of the latest snapshots. Update the opportunity tree and the list of assumptions.
3. **Continuously:** run small tests on the riskiest assumptions of the ideas you are considering.
4. **Regularly:** show stakeholders what you have learned and what you will test next.

## Start small

- Weekly interviewing is the keystone habit. If you have little support, begin with one interview a week and share what you learn.
- Underperforming features are a good moment to argue for discovery, because people are ready to listen.
- Adapt practices to your context. "That would never work here" usually means "that exact version would not work here". Find the smallest version that does.
- Build trust by showing results, not by explaining the method.
