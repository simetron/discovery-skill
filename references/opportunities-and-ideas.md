## The opportunity solution tree

An opportunity solution tree is a visual map with the desired outcome at the top, customer opportunities beneath it, and candidate solutions beneath those. It helps a team:
- break big opportunities into smaller ones,
- see how current work connects to the outcome,
- keep the team aligned and make the reasoning visible to stakeholders.

Build it from your interview snapshots and experience map: pull out needs, pains and desires in the customers' words, group them by the moments in the experience, and nest related ones under a parent. Expect a first version in well under a day, then revise it often as you learn.

## Rules for good opportunities

An opportunity is a customer need, pain or desire.

- Word it from the customer's perspective, not the company's.
- Make it specific and common across customers, and capable of moving your outcome.
- If there is only one way to address it, it is a solution in disguise. A real opportunity has more than one possible solution.
- A feeling is not an opportunity. Look for what causes it.
- Top-level branches represent distinct moments and must not overlap.
- No opportunity has two parents. Avoid long chains with a single child at every level.
- Parent-child links break big opportunities into smaller ones; siblings group similar items.

## Prioritizing opportunities

Work one level at a time:
1. Compare the top-level opportunities and choose the most promising one.
2. Repeat inside the chosen branch until you reach a leaf you can act on.

Compare siblings on four lenses:

| Lens | Question |
|---|---|
| Size | How many customers are affected, and how often? |
| Market | Would solving it differentiate you or is it table stakes? |
| Company | Does it fit your strategy and the team's capabilities? |
| Customer | How important is it to them, and how satisfied are they with current solutions? |

Don't score numerically. It is a reasoned, subjective comparison informed by data. Choosing an opportunity is reversible because you are only committing to explore it further, so do not wait for perfect information.

Focus on one target opportunity at a time. Keep interviewing after you choose, because the tree will change.

## Generating solutions

- Have each person generate ideas alone first, before any group discussion. Groups lose ideas to social loafing, conformity and one-at-a-time speaking, and give a false sense of productivity.
- Aim for volume: roughly 15 to 20 ideas per opportunity as a rule of thumb. Quantity first, judgment later, combine and improve ideas, welcome unusual ones.
- Borrow from analogous products in other industries and from extreme users.
- Helpful prompts: "What if we had a magic wand?" Take breaks, change scenery, sleep on it.
- Include diverse perspectives, including engineers and designers, from the start.
- Don't stop at variations of one idea, and don't limit ideation to a single session.

## Narrowing solutions

1. Share ideas as a team.
2. Remove ideas that do not address the target opportunity.
3. Dot-vote down to about three candidates, each with a real advocate on the team.
4. Compare the candidates against each other. Never ask "should we do this one idea?"; always ask "which of these is best for the opportunity?"

Holding several candidates guards against committing too early to a favorite. Move on to listing their assumptions (see `assumptions-and-testing.md`).
